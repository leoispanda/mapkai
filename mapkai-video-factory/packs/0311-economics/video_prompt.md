## Global MapKAI Creative Direction

# MapKAI Field Overview — Master Creative Direction

Create an intellectually compelling overview for an intelligent adult learner without specialist training. The goal is not encyclopedic coverage. The goal is a correct, memorable mental map of what a field is trying to understand and why its methods became necessary.

This is neither a conventional educational explainer, a textbook recap, a lecture, nor a list of branches and definitions. The intended experience is **documentary × intellectual fable × guided journey through a knowledge map**. Lead the viewer through questions and discoveries; do not present a syllabus.

## Narrative principle

Open with a concrete mystery, paradox, dilemma, observation, discovery, intellectual conflict, or thought experiment that contains something fundamental about the field. Do not start by defining the discipline. Let curiosity exist briefly before the explanation reveals why people needed an entire field to address the problem.

The narrative must earn its place. It should create a cognitive experience that leads to understanding, not decorative drama. Prefer real discoveries, experiments, historical conflicts, and clearly labelled thought experiments over invented characters, classroom dialogue, or cartoon-like scenes.

## Intellectual reversal

Every overview needs one genuine reversal: an apparent certainty becomes more complicated, or a familiar event acquires a new explanation. The desired reaction is: “Wait — that changes how I see it.” This must disclose the field's logic, not merely provide trivia.

## Knowledge responsibility

Narrative quality never substitutes for intellectual completeness. Integrate, organically rather than as headings read aloud:

- the central question and the human tension behind it;
- why the field emerged and how its characteristic reasoning works;
- its major intellectual territories and landmark shifts;
- real-world consequences and cross-field relationships;
- limits, open questions, and a final mental model.

When explaining a landmark, name the assumption that changed. Do not only name a person, theory, or date. Use history as: old model → anomaly → new idea → changed worldview. Present branches as different territories that became necessary because parts of the central question demanded different tools.

## Tone and visual thinking

The tone is mature, calm, elegant, curious, accessible, and intellectually serious. Accessibility must not become simplification. Prefer one genuinely understood idea to ten shallow facts.

Avoid children's-story narration, cute invented characters, fake excitement, cheesy motivation, generic encyclopedia openings, mechanical enumeration, and shallow everyday-life examples. Avoid unsupported quotations, statistics, historical anecdotes, and claims of a single founder.

Use visuals to explain: scale shifts, experiments, diagrams, timelines, maps, before/after conceptual models, physical processes, systems, and relationships. Decorative imagery is not a substitute for visual reasoning.

## Ending

Return to the opening question, now transformed by the journey. End with one compact intellectual insight—not a call to action, recap, or branding line. The viewer should feel both: “That was genuinely interesting” and “I now understand what this field is fundamentally trying to do.”


## Field-Specific Narrative Blueprint

**Selected narrative engine:** paradox

**Central device:** A city wakes to find every wallet contains twice as much money, yet no extra homes, food, doctors, labour, or productive capacity exist.

**Opening:** Begin with the apparent good news: everyone has twice as much money. Let the question hang—has the city become twice as rich?

**Tension:** Money measures and coordinates claims on resources, but it is not the resources themselves. The paradox reveals scarcity, distribution, and institutions.

**First turn:** If available goods and capacities have not changed, extra money changes claims and prices rather than automatically doubling real wellbeing.

**Aha moment:** Economics begins not with greed or arithmetic but with opportunity cost: every allocation uses resources that cannot simultaneously do something else.

**Progression:** From scarcity and choice, move to prices and markets, then public goods and externalities, macroeconomic coordination, and institutions that shape who receives what.

**Knowledge integration:** Use microeconomics for local choices, macroeconomics for economy-wide coordination, public economics for shared goods, and behavioural and institutional economics for real human decision-making. Connect to politics, finance, management, and statistics at the points where rules, capital, organisations, and evidence enter.

**Ending return:** Return to the doubled wallets. The city was asking not how much money people held, but how a society turns limited productive capacity into lives people can actually live.

**Final insight:** Economics maps the trade-offs, incentives, and institutions that turn scarce resources into social outcomes.

## Mandatory Coverage Requirements

Field identity and boundary: Economics studies how people, organisations, and societies allocate scarce resources among competing uses—and how the resulting choices shape production, exchange, income, power, and wellbeing.

Central question: When every useful choice has an opportunity cost, how do individual decisions become prices, markets, institutions, and social outcomes?

Why this field exists: Resources, time, information, and attention are limited, while needs and ambitions are not. Economics exists to make the trade-offs visible, including the trade-offs hidden inside rules, markets, and public policy.

How it thinks: Economics starts from incentives and constraints, then examines how choices interact. It combines models, data, causal inference, historical comparison, and institutional analysis. Its strongest habit is to ask: compared with what alternative, for whom, and over what time horizon?

Major branches to integrate:
- Microeconomics — households, firms, prices, competition, and market design.
- Macroeconomics — growth, inflation, employment, money, and business cycles.
- Public economics — taxation, public goods, welfare, and collective choices.
- Development and labour economics — productivity, inequality, work, and long-run change.
- Behavioural and institutional economics — bounded decision-making and the rules that shape markets.

Landmark ideas to explain as shifts:
- Opportunity cost: choosing one use of a resource means giving up another.
- Supply and demand: prices coordinate dispersed decisions but do not automatically make outcomes fair.
- Comparative advantage: exchange can create gains even when one side is more productive in everything.
- Externalities and public goods: private choices can impose costs or create benefits that prices miss.
- Incentives and institutions: rules change behaviour, and behaviour changes the outcomes rules can sustain.

Historical shift: Economics evolved from moral and political reflection on trade and wealth to classical political economy, marginal analysis, national income accounting, Keynesian macroeconomics, modern econometrics, behavioural research, and renewed attention to institutions, inequality, and climate constraints.

Applications:
- pricing and competition policy
- central banking and inflation analysis
- tax, welfare, and public investment
- labour markets and organisational decisions
- climate and environmental policy

Cross-field connections:
- Finance, banking and insurance (0412) applies economic reasoning to capital and risk.
- Management and administration (0413) studies organisational choices within firms.
- Political sciences and civics (0312) explains how collective rules are made.
- Statistics (0542) helps distinguish correlation, causation, and uncertainty in economic evidence.

Limits and frontiers: Clarify assumptions, incomplete evidence, contested interpretations, and the need for neighbouring fields.

Final mental model: A general learner should leave able to see an economic argument as a claim about trade-offs, incentives, distribution, and institutions—not merely a chart about money.
